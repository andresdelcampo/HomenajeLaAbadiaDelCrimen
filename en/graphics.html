How the abbey is drawn

From a world coordinate to a complete room: a visual guide to room descriptions, architectural recipes, tiles, layers, and masks that turn a small amount of data into a monumental building.

01 · The essential idea

The screen takes shape from a description

The fixed setting is generated by interpreting a compact description. The program chooses a room, reads its parts, executes each part’s drawing recipe, and composites the resulting tiles. Characters, doors, and objects arrive later.

Scope and reliability

What exactly are we looking at?

The central explanation follows the original Amstrad CPC system, checked against its reconstructed code: room selection, descriptions, recipes, grids, depth, tiles, masks, and palette. The plates make that process visible, but do not claim to reproduce a complete frame with characters and objects. Readable algorithm and variable names were created for this explanation.

02 · From monastery to view

Three coordinate spaces

World, construction, and screen operate at different scales. Following the passage from one to the next makes the system much easier to understand.

1. The world cell

Characters move in global coordinates. Dividing both horizontal coordinates by sixteen reduces the position to the cell containing the camera. Height selects the ground floor, first floor, or library.

The parity of the two coordinates—whether each cell is odd or even—also determines the isometric orientation. That combination alone lets adjacent cells face the building from alternating directions.

cell_x ← position_x DIV 16
cell_y ← position_y DIV 16

if height is below the first boundary: floor ← GROUND
if height lies between both boundaries: floor ← FIRST
if height exceeds the second boundary: floor ← LIBRARY

x_parity ← cell_x MOD 2
y_parity ← cell_y MOD 2
orientation ← combination of x_parity and y_parity

view ← view_map[floor, cell_x, cell_y]

2. The construction grid

Recipes draw onto a local grid larger than the screen. Every tile is placed relative to the origin of the block that produced it. Position, height, and orientation determine its destination.

Each cell provides a rear slot and a front slot. This pair records which architecture belongs behind the characters and which part may pass in front of them.

FOR EACH tile emitted by a recipe:
    destination_x ← origin_x + offset_x
    destination_y ← origin_y + offset_y
    depth ← relative position of the destination and its height

    if the destination lies inside the grid:
        place tile in the rear or front slot
        according to depth

3. The screen window

Once every recipe has finished, the program crops a window sixteen columns wide and twenty rows high from the grid. The margins mark the beginning of that visible area.

Every tile measures 16 × 8 pixels, so the architectural background occupies 256 × 160 pixels. The program visits each cell in order and converts its rear tile, followed by its front tile, into pixels for the selected machine.

FOR row FROM 0 TO 19:
    FOR column FROM 0 TO 15:
        construction_x ← column + margin_x
        construction_y ← row + margin_y
        cell ← grid[construction_y, construction_x]

        draw the rear tile of cell
        draw the front tile of cell
        retain its depth information
03 · The grammar of stone

A block is a small drawing program

A room is described by a list of placements. Each one selects an architectural recipe, fixes its origin, supplies up to two parameters, and—when needed—a height for occlusion ordering. A few values can therefore encode a complex image.

A description that occupies only what it needs

Rooms do not reserve the same amount of space. Each description declares its length, contains as many placements as it needs, and ends with a terminator. To locate a room, the program advances through the lengths of the preceding descriptions.

A placement packs the block type, two local coordinates, and two small parameters into three bytes. It adds a fourth only when an initial height is required. This economy avoids storing a complete grid for every view: two flat 16 × 20 maps for all 116 slots would require at least 74,240 bytes; all the descriptions occupy 9,014.

The grammar

Four recipes, four scales

The same machinery can raise an arcade, extend a staircase, open a row of windows, or lengthen a wall. Parameters change the result without requiring an entirely new drawing.

Recipes with variable dimensions

A recipe begins with a small set of values: the tiles it commonly uses, its drawing cursor, and the parameters it has received. It then advances through a sequence of simple instructions.

It can emit a run, move the cursor, repeat according to a requested width or height, save and restore a position, mirror the horizontal axis, change a value, or invoke another recipe. The same definition can therefore produce a short or long wall, a staircase of varying extent, or an arcade facing the other way.

CONCEPTUAL EXAMPLE — NOT A TRANSCRIBED RECIPE

RECIPE wall_with_arches(origin, length, height):
    cursor ← origin
    REPEAT length TIMES:
        EMIT pillar_tile AT cursor
        MOVE cursor RIGHT

        REPEAT height TIMES:
            EMIT arch_tile AT cursor
            MOVE cursor UP

        RESTORE cursor TO BASE
        MIRROR vocabulary IF orientation_changes

    END recipe

What those words mean: the cursor is the cell that will receive the next tile; emit means writing that tile into the grid; restore the base returns the cursor to the foot of the structure; and mirror reverses the orientation so the recipe can face the other way. The example above teaches the language’s possibilities; it does not reproduce one particular historical recipe. Each plate records one representative execution: parameters can change its length, height, orientation, and tile combination.

Look closerThe recipe interpreter

Each type leads to a definition with two parts: a local vocabulary of twelve values—normally tile identifiers and constants—and the instruction sequence. At the start, the two placement parameters, optional height, and two depth coordinates are added: seventeen working values in all, alongside a cursor and a small stack.

The interpreter can emit and move, repeat and restore, calculate, transform coordinates, and invoke another recipe. Tile runs have their own shorthand: an instruction can emit a piece, advance, repeat it, and continue to the next main instruction. Floors, steps, and long walls can therefore be described with very little data.

04 · One room from beginning to end

Let us follow room 17

One continuous example joins all the abstractions. The number 17 is a hexadecimal identifier: here it names a view, not a coordinate or an ordinary decimal count.

The floor and world cell lead to identifier 17.

02Description

Its record contains 32 placements using 22 distinct block types.

03Execution

Together, 1,899 instructions are interpreted to emit floors, walls, stairs, columns, and railings.

04Ordering

Tiles meeting in one cell are compared through two depth coordinates and enter the rear or front slot.

05Composition

The initial colour, masks, and two tiles in each cell produce the finished architectural background.

An unusually demanding check

This room connects the entire chain recovered in the CPC code: view selection, the reading of 32 placements, recipe execution, tile choice, depth calculation, masks, initial colour, and final composition.

It is a useful test because no stage can be correct in isolation. A mistake in interpreting one placement or ordering one tile propagates through arches, stairs, columns, and railings, and no longer produces the coherent architecture shown here.

05 · Depth and transparency

Two tiles can occupy the same cell

An isometric setting must know what belongs behind and what belongs in front. Every visible position therefore keeps two architectural slots. Optional height and two depth values derived from position help insert each tile in the correct order.

The visual stack

LAYER 1Rear architecture: floor, wall, or another element behind.
ACTORSCharacters, doors, and objects are later mixed with the setting.
LAYER 2Front architecture that may hide part of an actor.

    composite rear tile

    restore the altered background
    draw actors and objects
    retain occluding elements in front of them

Two related but different orders: the stack on the left explains the visual result. The algorithm on the right separates the pass that prepares the architecture from the dynamic composition of characters and objects; an actor is not literally inserted between both tiles while each background cell is being constructed.

Two coordinates determine depth

When a placement supplies height, the system combines it with local position. The result is not a modern depth value for every pixel, but two values attached to the emitted tile. Recipes may modify them as they advance.

When several architectural pieces reach one cell, these values help retain one behind and another in front. Each visible position therefore stores two complete records: depth plus tile.

half_height ← height DIV 2

depth_a ← tile_y + half_height + tile_x − 15
depth_b ← tile_y + half_height − tile_x + 16

compare the new tile’s depth
with the rear and front slots

A mask decides what survives

Darkness and transparency are separate properties. Every graphic fragment contributes both colour and a mask. The mask decides which bits of the old background survive; the colour contributes the new ones. A diagonal edge can therefore reveal the floor while an opaque black area still paints black.

Treating “colour zero” as universally transparent opens false wedges and holes in arches, stairs, and walls. The colour used to fill the view before drawing also becomes part of the final result.

FOR EACH encoded_fragment OF tile:
    mask   ← mask corresponding to encoded_fragment
    colour ← colour corresponding to state and encoded_fragment

    destination ← (destination AND mask) OR colour
Look closerFrom an encoded tile to sixteen pixels

Each CPC tile contains eight rows of four bytes: 32 bytes in all. In the graphic mode used here, each byte encodes four pixels; unpacking it yields sixteen pixels per row and four possible colour indices. The upper half of the 256 identifiers selects the second pair of composition tables.

For every pixel, the colour already on screen is read first. One table then decides which part survives and another what the new tile contributes; the two are combined into the result. Day and night do not alter these data or their masks: they replace the four colours assigned to the same indices.

06 · Day, night, and version

The palette is logic too

In the original system, night replaces the four-colour palette without changing geometry or tiles. Other conversions handle colour differently, as the comparison below explains.

The same geometry, read differently

Pixel indices, masks, and tile identifiers remain stable between day and night; what changes is the palette that turns the four indices into visible colours. Actual darkness later introduces a dynamic limited light system; the night plates in this guide keep the architecture fully exposed so it can be studied.

The header selector compares five graphic versions through their vocabulary and geometry. The comparison is visual: each conversion has its own internal representation, and only behaviour verified as common is attributed to all of them.

if moment IS day:
    palette ← four daytime colours
    background ← lit base index
else:
    palette ← four night-time colours
    background ← night-time base index

FOR EACH tile:
    pixels ← unpack its encoded indices
    apply its masks and colours over background

Night does not necessarily mean darkness

The time of day changes the palette, but a lit room retains the same index for its background. Separately, a room may be marked as dark: the view then starts from black, and the dynamic light system determines what area becomes visible.

The night plates on this page are therefore palette studies with the complete architecture exposed. They do not represent how much the player can actually see inside a dark area.

if the moment IS night:
    install night colours
    use the night palette's base index

if the room IS ALSO dark:
    start from a black background
    dynamic lighting determines
    which part becomes visible
07 · The map that cannot be seen

Height is built separately

The architectural picture and the floor characters walk on occupy separate but related structures. A second description fills a local 24 × 24 grid of values with rectangles and ramps.

Geometry for movement and occlusion

Every cell stores a height. Flat areas receive a constant value; stairs and slopes advance by adding or subtracting height along one direction. The window covers the 16 × 16 world cell and adds a four-unit margin on every side, producing its 24 × 24 positions.

Its function is geometric: the grid decides whether a character may advance, must climb or descend, and how it should be ordered against parts of the setting. Drawing the background remains the task of the architectural recipes.

empty height_grid[24, 24]
origin ← current_cell × 16 − margin

FOR EACH shape IN floor_geometry:
    clipped ← part of shape inside local_window

    if shape IS landing:
        fill clipped with initial_height
    if shape IS ramp:
        height ← initial_height
        FOR EACH step ALONG ramp_direction:
            store height in the cell
            height ← height + slope
Look closerFive shapes construct the relief

Each shape carries an initial height, a position, and two dimensions. One of the five classes fills a rectangle at constant height. The other four make ramps: height rises or falls one step at a time along one of the two directions of the plan.

The two dimensions may be packed together or stored separately with one additional unit. Before writing, every shape is clipped against the local window. One continuous floor description can therefore contribute only the portion surrounding the current room.

08 · What the fixed picture omits

The setting awaits its actors

At the end of this process, the architecture and its height geometry are ready. Dynamic layers are then added to complete the scene remembered by the player.

Later dynamic layers

Characters, doors, objects, animation, reflections, background restoration, and the circle of light in dark areas belong to later phases. The parchment, text, and lower status panel also follow independent drawing paths.

This separation is an advantage: the fixed room can be built once and retained while actors move. Only the areas touched by sprites need to be restored and composited again.

if room_changes:
    fixed_background ← result of steps 1 to 7 in this guide

ON EVERY FRAME:
    recover the background beneath last frame's actors
    update characters, doors, and objects
    order elements by position, height, and layers
    draw the dynamic areas
    if room_is_dark:
        restrict the visible area to the lamp's range
Look closerWhy the background appears from the centre

Once the grid is full, CPC drawing does not simply visit its rows from top to bottom. It starts near the centre and follows strips down, right, up, and left. Each circuit lengthens the strips, forming a rectangular spiral until all 16 × 20 visible cells have been covered.

This order determines how the reconstruction appears while it is being drawn, but does not alter the final result: the rear tile is composited before the front tile in every cell. Each tile is then unpacked as eight consecutive rows of sixteen pixels, preserving or replacing the previous colour according to its mask tables.

How we know it fits

Evidence, boundaries, and terminology

Figures, formats, and algorithms presented as facts come from coherent structures, complete execution of every description, and visual comparison. The readable names of operations, recipes, and variables are ours: they describe function without attributing historical vocabulary to the programmers.

  • Structural check. All 116 descriptions can be followed to their ends, and the known instructions execute without encountering an unknown operation.
  • Code cross-check. Room selection, the interpreter, depth formulas, two layers, masks, and the centre-outward traversal appear together in the CPC reconstruction.
  • A catalogue with exceptions. Of the 87 non-null definitions, five are not called by any room description; three of those finish without emitting a tile.
  • Deliberate boundary. This account reconstructs fixed architecture and height geometry. Actors, doors, objects, reflections, restoration, limited lighting, parchment, text, and status panel belong to other systems.

This is enough to implement an independent background reconstructor: first the world map and rooms, then the block interpreter, next layers and masks, and finally—as separate projects—actors, objects, and dynamic lighting.
