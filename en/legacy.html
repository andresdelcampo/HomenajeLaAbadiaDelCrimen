XIII · Two reconstructions

First rebuild; then expand

Antonio Giner carried the intact abbey onto modern computers and redrew it in VGA. Years later, Manuel Pazos and Daniel Celemín used that memory as a starting point for opening new rooms.

2001–08 · VGA / 32-bit

The same abbey, in 256 colours

Antonio Giner’s remake grew from a reverse-engineered reconstruction of the PC executable. A VGA DOS edition began circulating in 2001; the Windows adaptation restored sound, added translations, and brought the game to 32-bit systems. The build preserved in this project is dated 30 December 2008.

Map, schedule, characters, and rules remain intact. The work centres on presentation and compatibility: the scenery is redrawn in 256 colours, although the program can also return to the colour or monochrome CPC artwork.

2016 · Free

Extensum: remake and expansion

Extensum preserves the isometric heart and schedule, but enlarges the map, adds characters, situations, cinematics, and new logic. It offers classic or directional controls and optional aids: map, alternate camera, an indicator for the sleeping Abbot, and an oil gauge.

Its lineage is long. Manuel Pazos had studied and ported the work for years—MSX2, J2ME, Java—while Daniel Celemín renewed the visuals with pixel art that evokes the film without abandoning the original’s clarity.

It runs on Windows, macOS, and Linux with several interface languages. More than 20,000 copies were downloaded during its first week; Steam reviews remain very positive.

A cult work, literally

In 2017 Correos marked the thirtieth anniversary with six hexagonal stamps in a parchment-shaped miniature sheet. Warm its thermochromic ink and objects appeared behind doors—a physical effect uncannily true to a game about looking where one must not.

The issue began as an initiative by the Association of Classic Computer Users (AUIC). Its account of the process preserves the petition, initial rejection, appeal, sketches, and collective effort that culminated in a design by Juan Delcán, co-creator of the game’s graphics.

Retro Gamer España published part one of “Paco Menéndez ante el espejo” in issue 40, under the subtitle “El genio de Avilés que quería saber.” Issue 41 continued the profile with “De los monjes franciscanos a la red neuronal de PALOMA,” also promoted on its cover. The eleven-page extract from issue 41 is preserved locally and presented above; the pages of part one remain to be located.

The abbey survives because every generation finds a different door: cassette, emulator, code comment, stamp, remake.
17 November 2017 · UPM

An abbey one could enter

AUIC and the Technical University of Madrid inaugurated La Abadía del Crimen, el lado humano del videojuego at the Historical Museum of Computing, presenting it as Spain’s first permanent exhibition devoted to a video game.

The installation turned isometric perspective into physical space: a small labyrinth structured as a seven-day visit, bringing together the game’s technical and human history. The official presentation of the stamp and its ceremonial cancellation took place at the same event.

La Vozde Avilés
Letters to the Editor11 August 2010

A citizen remembers

A street for Paco Menéndez

José Manuel Braña Álvarez

The first time I loaded La Abadía del Crimen on a Sinclair Spectrum, I realised I was facing a video game unlike any I had enjoyed before. I think almost every child who experienced home computing in the eighties—whether its surname was MSX, Amstrad, Commodore, or Spectrum—shares that memory. La Abadía was a challenge. Something beyond us. Its characters interacted with one another as we had never seen before, amid settings of enormous complexity and detail. It was too much for us, far too much.

Paco was a genius; that is how he is remembered. One need only visit a few websites to grasp his importance in the history of Spanish software, and the influence and new programming vocations he inspired. He is the symbol of an era that already has a name: “The Golden Age of Spanish Software.”

Even today, reading old interviews with Menéndez or recollections by colleagues who worked with him, such as Juan Delcán, conveys an infectious enthusiasm. The visionary drive of pioneers, of geniuses. And, above all, his humility.

In one of those interviews, conducted when he announced that he was leaving video games after achieving success and receiving awards for his work—he was at the top—he said humbly: “I prefer people’s recognition to money.”

We should proudly give him that recognition here, in Avilés, his home town. A street. A tribute.

His final project was a new computer system based on a new kind of intelligent matrix memory that he was researching. Paco Menéndez wanted to change the world with everything he did, even though it cost him his life and deprived us of his talent early. Far too early.

English translation of the letter published in La Voz de Avilés. No scan of the original page has been located; its Spanish text survives in the later civic petition, which reached its goal with 590 signatures.
